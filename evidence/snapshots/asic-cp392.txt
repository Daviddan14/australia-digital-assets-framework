About this paper
This consultation paper seeks feedback on our implementation of the
Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 (DAF Act)
and anticipated regulations and amendments (subject to Government
processes).
It outlines our proposals on the preliminary draft standards, proposed
modifications and relief under the Corporations Act, and draft regulatory
guidance.
Note 1: This consultation refers to regulatory design work that is being developed by
Government in parallel to this process. This is intended to enable industry to see a
complete picture of the future approach.
Note 2: The preliminary draft standards and draft Regulatory Guide 000 Digital asset
platforms and tokenised custody platforms (draft RG 000), which are attached to this
paper, are available for download from the CP 392 landing page. These are attached for
illustrative purposes only and the final form of the instruments may change considerably
from the preliminary drafts.

Background to the proposals

Key points
The Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026 (DAF
Act) introduces a legislative regime for the regulation of entities offering
financial products called digital asset platforms (DAPs) and tokenised
custody platforms (TCPs).
The DAF Act commences on 8 April 2027. If you are currently operating a
platform that will be considered a DAP or TCP on 8 April 2027, you must
apply for an Australian financial services (AFS) licence or variation by
7 October 2027. You can continue operating while we assess your
application.
We will implement these changes according to our roadmap, including
transitional arrangements and standard making.
We will publish regulatory guidance to support the implementation of the
new regime, including guidance on licensing, conduct, custody, disclosure,
and operational standards.
This consultation paper describes and invites comment on our proposed
approach to regulatory guidance, standards and financial requirements for
DAPs and TCPs.

The Digital Assets Framework Act
1

On 1 April 2026, Parliament passed the DAF Act. It received Royal Assent
on 8 April 2026.

Purpose of the DAF Act
2

The DAF Act implements the Government’s commitment to modernise
Australia’s digital asset regulatory regime.

3

The DAF Act addresses regulatory gaps and consequent uncertainty
regarding digital assets, and some infrastructure and arrangements that
support them. It aims to foster innovation, enhance competition and growth,
and strengthen Australia’s position in the global digital asset ecosystem.

4

The DAF Act amends the Corporations Act to:
(a)

regulate certain parts of the digital asset market by creating two new
financial products—DAPs and TCPs;

(b)

set out the licensing regime for DAPs and TCPs, which will be
administered by ASIC, including how these two products interact with
other products and services subject to the Corporations Act;

(c)

set out the core obligations for entities providing financial services in
relation to DAPs and TCPs;

(d)

provide ASIC with specific powers to set standards in relation to assetholding and transactional and settlement activities;

(e)

provide the Minister with powers to deem whether a facility is a DAP,
financial market or a clearing and settlement facility; and

(f)

introduce some targeted carve-outs from the existing financial services
laws for certain digital asset related arrangements.

The DAF Act also amends the Australian Securities and Investments
Commission Act 2001 (ASIC Act) to:
(a)

introduce DAPs and TCPs as financial products;

(b)

extend existing requirements, prohibitions and ASIC powers with
respect to DAPs and TCPs; and

(c)

amend definitions to ensure that the regulatory treatment of new
concepts is consistent with the amendments to the Corporations Act.

Interactions with the payments reforms
6

The Government is also undertaking work to modernise the regulatory
regime for payment services providers (payments reforms). Treasury
consulted on draft legislation for Tranche 1 of the payments reforms in
March and April 2026.

7

The payments reforms are designed to complement, but operate
independently from, the DAF Act. There will be separate regulation of
certain stablecoins as tokenised stored value facilities (tokenised SVFs). Any
ASIC regulatory guidance on tokenised SVFs will be provided as part of
guidance on the payments reforms in due course.

International regulatory developments
8

Several overseas jurisdictions have already implemented comprehensive
regulatory regimes for digital/crypto assets, including European Union
members (through the Markets in Crypto-Assets Regulation (MiCA)),
Singapore and Hong Kong. Some others, including the United Kingdom, are
in the process of implementing significant reform packages and developing
further reform measures. The DAF Act supports Australia’s alignment with
global approaches.

9

These international regulatory developments followed the 2023
recommendations from the International Organization of Securities

ASIC’s role as regulator of DAPs and TCPs
11

Under the new regime, ASIC is responsible for administering the regulatory
requirements for DAPs and TCPs under the Corporations Act.

12

Our role includes:
(a)

assessing applications for, and granting, AFS licences authorising the
operation of DAPs and TCPs;

(b)

monitoring compliance with the obligations that apply to AFS licensees,
including operators of DAPs and TCPs;

(c)

making legislative instruments and setting standards that apply to DAPs
and TCPs, including asset-holding standards and transactional and
settlement standards; and

(d)

taking enforcement action where there are breaches of the law.

13

Our aim is to promote confident and informed participation by consumers,
maintain fair and orderly markets, and reduce risks associated with the
custody, transfer and use of digital assets.

14

The new regime cannot (and is not seeking to) remove all risks associated
with buying and selling digital assets. Many digital assets are high-risk
speculative investments and will remain high-risk under this regime (even
once DAPs and TCPs are regulated). As with other high-risk investments,
consumers should be aware that they could lose the entire value of their
investment.

15

We are developing regulatory guidance to help industry understand and
comply with the new obligations (see Part 1 below). This guidance will
cover licensing, general obligations, custody, market integrity, disclosure,
token governance, operational resilience and other areas where digital asset
activities introduce novel risks.

ASIC’s implementation objectives
16

We are taking a principles-based approach that focuses on regulatory
outcomes rather than prescriptive operational requirements, where possible.

17

The guidance, standards, modifications and relief we propose to make, as
outlined in this consultation paper, are anchored in the following objectives:
(a)

the globally adopted ‘same activity, same risk, same
regulation/regulatory outcome’ approach that mirrors other IOSCO
members and supports international consistency in the digital asset
space;

(b)

standards and obligations should be principles-based, proportional, riskbased and technology neutral; and

(c)

avoiding potential regulatory arbitrage while also recognising the
unique arrangements, benefits and risks of the digital assets and the
underlying technology.

18

This approach provides industry with clarity on expectations while
preserving flexibility and avoiding unnecessarily prescriptive rules that may
quickly become outdated in the rapidly evolving digital asset environment.

19

This approach also provides consistency between digital assets and
traditional financial products and services, which is important as traditional
financial services firms begin to offer both new and existing products in
digital asset form, and as digital asset firms increasingly look to provide
traditional financial products and services.

20

A principles-based and risk-based approach means that not all questions can,
or should, be answered in our guidance or standards. Firms will need to
determine how they will apply the standards and guidance. In some cases,
this may mean working with industry bodies to determine appropriate ways
to apply the principles (e.g. as good practice emerges). This is consistent
with our approach to traditional financial products and services.

21

Importantly, the ‘same activity, same risk, same regulation/regulatory
outcome’ does not mean identical application of regulation in all cases. We
expect that we will implement different regulatory requirements, and firms
could employ differing compliance arrangements, to achieve the same
regulatory outcome where it can be demonstrated that:

22

(a)

the risk profile differs;

(b)

the activity is materially different; or

(c)

there are specific features of the product, service or support technology
that require a bespoke approach.

We continue to seek feedback from stakeholders on where the risks in
relation to DAPs and TCPs differ from traditional financial products and

Current state of play
23

industry with clarity, certainty and sufficient time to adapt systems, controls
and governance to meet the new requirements.

A

Regulatory guidance relating to DAPs and
TCPs

Key points
We propose to provide regulatory guidance on key concepts and
obligations introduced by the new legislation for DAPs and TCPs across
both the primary law and the anticipated regulations (subject to
Government processes): see draft Regulatory Guide 000 Digital asset
platforms and tokenised custody platforms (draft RG 000) at Attachment 1
to this paper.
We propose to provide licensing guidance tailored to digital asset business
models, including on new authorisations, new licensing exemptions, and
how general licensing obligations apply to DAPs and TCPs.
We propose to provide specific guidance on disclosure, including the
DAP/TCP Guide and platform rules.
The DAF Act, and anticipated regulations (subject to Government
processes), also makes amendments to the Corporations Act that do not
relate only to DAPs and TCPs. We intend to provide guidance on these
new parts of the Corporations Act in Information Sheet 225 Digital assets:
Financial products and services (INFO 225): see proposal E1.

General approach to guidance
27

Consistent with ASIC’s broader regulatory simplification initiatives, we will
adopt a principles-based and outcomes-focused approach to guidance
regarding the DAP and TCP regime, supported by targeted guidance where
additional clarity is needed.

28

Guidance for operators of DAPs and TCPs will sit alongside and refer to
existing ASIC guidance on AFS licensing obligations. In other words, the
new regulatory guide will explain how we administer the DAP and TCP
regime without displacing or making significant changes to our existing
guidance.

guides for information about existing AFS licence obligations—see

Rationale
Dedicated regulatory guide for DAPs and TCPs
29

Digital asset markets develop rapidly. Embedding all guidance within
existing regulatory guides would require frequent amendments to
foundational documents that apply across the entire financial services
industry. By issuing a dedicated regulatory guide for DAPs and TCPs, we
can provide targeted updates, respond more quickly to emerging risks, and
avoid unnecessary disruption to long-standing guidance relied upon by other
sectors.

30

Our proposed guidance covers new concepts introduced by the DAF Act and
anticipated accompanying regulations (which are subject to Government
processes). Our guidance also comments on where these new laws may
result in material changes to the existing regulatory perimeter. We propose
this initial guidance as a starting point to support the industry, given the new
concepts introduced by the DAF Act. We also acknowledge that more
guidance may be needed over time as the industry and ASIC engage with
these concepts in practice.

31

Where established financial services principles continue to apply without
modification, we consider that existing ASIC guidance remains appropriate.

Retaining INFO 225 as primary guidance on when digital assets are
financial products
32

Retaining INFO 225 as the primary source of guidance on when digital
assets are, or are not, financial products supports continuity and consistency.
Industry is already familiar with INFO 225, and consolidating classification
guidance into a single location reduces fragmentation and the risk of
conflicting interpretations. This approach also ensures that any new guidance
specific to DAPs and TCPs can focus on operational, conduct, and licensing
issues without duplicating or re-writing existing classification principles.

33

DAP or TCP and how those concepts interact with other parts of the
financial services regime.

35

Draft RG 000 includes guidance on the important definitions supporting the
regime. These include, but are not limited to, digital tokens, DAPs and
TCPs, the extended meaning of dealing, exemptions, and framing within the
broader financial services regime.

36

Our proposed guidance explains the different financial services that can be
conducted in relation to DAPs and TCPs, including some exclusions for
certain DAPs and TCPs classified as other financial products or services. It
can be complex to navigate the various combinations of products and
services offered; although we cannot be comprehensive, the guidance sets
out the most common combinations we have observed.

37

The complexity also extends beyond the AFS licensing regime and into how
the DAP and TCP regime interacts with the financial markets and clearing
and settlement facility regimes. Again, while these provisions are new, we
consider it important to set out our understanding of how the regimes
interact, and how the Minister’s deeming powers apply. For example, a
facility cannot be both a DAP (as a financial product) and a financial market
or clearing and settlement (CS) facility simultaneously: see draft RG
000.60–RG 000.62.

38

The DAF Act and anticipated accompanying regulations (subject to
Government processes) introduce several exemptions for DAPs and TCPs,
and the general exemptions that apply to AFS licensees continue to apply to
AFS licensees with DAP or TCP authorisations. These exemptions are
important to outline as they go to considerations for when a licence is
required. New licensing exemptions listed in draft RG 000 include a:
(a)

licensing exemption for low value DAPs;

(b)

limited advice and arranging exemption;

(c)

third-party asset-holding arrangements exemption;

(d)

overseas issuer and client exemption;

(e)

overseas related body corporate or joint venture exemption; and

(f)

nominee company exemption.
Note: See draft RG 000.75–RG 000.77.

Licensing authorisation guidance
39

The DAF Act introduces two new financial products—DAPs and TCPs. As a
result, from 8 October 2027, DAP and TCP operators (and certain other
financial services providers) will require an appropriate DAP or TCP-related
licence authorisation to carry on a financial services business in Australia,
unless covered by a licensing exemption (including those in anticipated
accompanying regulations, subject to Government processes). Different
authorisations will be required depending on a person’s business model.

B

Asset-holding standards

Key points
We propose to make asset-holding standards that establish minimum
requirements for how underlying assets and money are held by DAP or
TCP operators.
The aim of the proposed standards is to ensure the appropriate
safeguarding, identification and administration of underlying assets and
money. The standards apply principles-based obligations informed by
existing regulatory models to DAP and TCP operators performing assetholding functions.
The proposed asset-holding standards include requirements regarding:
• governance and organisational arrangements
• arrangements for holding underlying assets and money
• identification, safeguarding, segregation and pooling
• receiving and acting on client instructions
• redemption and delivery rights and controls
• use of underlying assets
• wallet and address information
• digital token identifiers, and
• arrangements with custodians.

Proposal to make asset-holding standards
60

Under s912BE of the Corporations Act, ASIC has the power to make assetholding standards that deal with specific matters relating to DAPs and TCPs.
The purpose of these standards, as stated in paragraph 1.170 of the
Explanatory Memorandum, is to establish minimum requirements for assetholding functions of these platforms.

61

Section 912BE of the Corporations Act sets out the matters that may be
addressed by the standards, and the matters that ASIC must be reasonably
satisfied of before making a standard. The Government has indicated that
these requirements are modelled on existing regimes for comparable assetholding arrangements, with additional considerations for holding money and
de-banking risks.
Note: See paragraphs 1.170 and 1.175 of the Explanatory Memorandum.

Transactional and settlement standards

Key points
We propose to make transactional and settlement standards to establish
minimum requirements for how client transactions are executed and settled
on, or through, a DAP.
The aim of the proposed standards is to provide clients with confidence and
certainty, by applying principles-based obligations from existing regulatory
models, in a proportionate way, to DAP operators engaging in transactional
activities.
The proposed transactional and settlement standards include requirements
relating to:
• platform governance
• asset admission and conflicts management
• trade controls, monitoring and intervention
• order matching and settlement arrangements
• execution platforms
• trade transparency
• client instructions
• trade confirmations
• overarching adequacy of arrangements to comply with the standards,
and
• overarching disclosure obligations.

Proposed transactional and settlement standards
84

Under s912BF of the Corporations Act, ASIC has the power to make
transactional and settlement standards. The purpose of these standards, as
stated in paragraph 1.178 of the Explanatory Memorandum, is to establish
minimum requirements for how client transactions are executed and settled
on, or through, a DAP.

85

Our assessment of the market is that most DAP operators will be required to
comply with the transactional and settlement standards, and some standards
may also apply to TCPs (see s912BB of the Corporations Act). Complying
with the transactional and settlement standards will be a core obligation for
DAP and TCP operators (see s912D(3) of the Corporations Act) for the
purposes of breach reporting. ASIC will be responsible for supervising the
compliance of DAP (and relevant TCP) operators with the standards.

106

When clients first interact with a DAP, it may appear similar to a traditional

differences between the business models of DAP operators. These
differences may not be readily apparent to clients and may affect their ability
to understand the nature of the service provided by the DAP operator and its
associated risks. We therefore propose that DAP operators are required to
provide clients with clear, concise and accessible information about these
matters before they first use the platform and on an ongoing basis thereafter.
This is intended to help clients make informed decisions about whether, and
how, to use the platform.

107

Disclosure of changes to platform rules, fees and third-party market making
arrangements supports the ability of investors to make informed decisions on
whether to continue using a platform. Given the industry’s fast pace of
innovation, it is likely that some DAP operators will implement material
changes to services more frequently than other traditional financial
equivalents.

108

Market makers and liquidity providers play an important role in providing
liquidity to a DAP, so that clients of the DAP can trade in the products that
are offered with less friction and tighter margins. Because of the importance
of this role, s912BF(3)(d) of the DAF Act requires a DAP operator to enter
an enforceable arrangement with any liquidity providers. Monitoring
obligations and public disclosure of a DAP’s arrangements with liquidity
providers supports transparency and helps address potential conflicts of
interest.

Rationale
148

This proposal aims to mitigate risks that arise where a licensee relies on a
stablecoin that it issues, that is issued by an associated entity, or that remains
under its custody or control. The value, liquidity and redeemability of the
stablecoin may depend on the financial position or operational resilience of
the licensee or its corporate group.

149

In these circumstances, the stablecoin’s effectiveness as an adequate
financial buffer may be affected by issues with the stablecoin’s value,
liquidity and redeemability due to the stablecoin’s lack of independence.

150

We therefore consider that additional safeguards are necessary where
stablecoins are used to satisfy NTA requirements. Prohibiting reliance on
‘restricted stablecoins’ will preserve the integrity of the financial
requirement regime by ensuring that recognised stablecoins operate as
independent liquidity resources.

151

This proposal only affects which stablecoins can be used as part of NTA
calculations. It does not affect when licensees can use ‘restricted stablecoins’
more generally in their business.

152

This proposal will apply to all AFS licensees, whether that requirement is
imposed by licence condition, legislative instrument or guidance. It is not
limited to licensees active in the digital asset industry. The proposal will
therefore apply to relevant instruments recently consulted on in Proposed
extension of financial services, credit and markets legislative instruments
(CS 62), notwithstanding that consultation’s proposal to only amend the selfrepeal date of each instrument.
