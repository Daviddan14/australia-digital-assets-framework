00

The short version

Five questions, answered in a sentence or two. The numbered markers open the exact words each answer rests on.

  • What does the Act do?

    It treats two kinds of custody business as financial products. In a digital asset platform, a business keeps digital tokens on behalf of others. In a tokenised custody platform, a business looks after a real asset and issues one token that stands for it1.

  • When does it start?

    On 8 April 202714. A business already running such a platform has until 7 October 2027 to apply for a licence, and can keep operating while ASIC decides4.

  • Who needs a licence?

    The operator of the platform, unless an exemption applies5. Small digital asset platforms are exempt if they hold no financial products, handle no more than $10 million of transactions a year, keep each client’s holdings within $5,000 (valued when tokens are added), and notify ASIC1.

  • What changes for issuers?

    Less than many expect. When a fund keeps its primary register of unit holders on chain, ASIC’s draft guide would usually treat it as a managed investment scheme rather than a tokenised custody platform5. The new rules bite hardest on whoever holds investors’ tokens or assets.

  • Where does Redbelly fit?

    Its identity and eligibility tools can enforce the kind of eligibility rules the Act asks platforms to set out, though the platform operator still has to make those rules transparent and non-discriminatory110. Whether the network counts as public digital token infrastructure, which sits outside licensing, is not settled14.

01

What the Act is, and why it exists

A short law with a large reach. It adds two financial products and leaves most of the detail to ASIC.

What the law says

Its full name is the Corporations Amendment (Digital Assets Framework) Act 2026, numbered No. 38 of 20261. Parliament passed it on 1 April 2026 and it received Royal Assent on 8 April 202614.

It amends the law on corporations and financial services: the Corporations Act 2001, and also the ASIC Act1.

Its central change is to add two new kinds of financial product: a digital asset platform and a tokenised custody platform, in each case unless the platform is a managed investment scheme1. If a facility is one of these and no exemption applies, its operator needs an Australian financial services (AFS) licence5.

When the Bill was introduced, the Government said it was meant to help unlock innovation and protect Australians investing in digital assets2. It pointed to a gap: a business could look after any amount of its clients’ digital assets with no financial-law safeguards in place, and collapses overseas had shown what weak oversight costs2.

ASIC describes the Act as addressing regulatory gaps and uncertainty about digital assets, with the aim of fostering innovation, competition and growth4. Its Commissioner summed up ASIC’s approach as “same activity, same risk, same regulatory outcome”3.

What this means

Read together, the definitions point to one idea: the licence follows custody. A business comes into the new rules by holding tokens, or holding real assets behind tokens, on behalf of other people. Using a blockchain, issuing a token or keeping your own tokens is not, by itself, what the definitions describe15.

The Act sets the frame and leaves the working detail to ASIC standards, ASIC guidance and Government regulations that are still being written. Most of that detail is due in 202745.

02

Six terms that do most of the work

The Act turns on a handful of defined words. These are the ones worth knowing.

What the law says

Key terms in the Act
TermWhat it meansSection
Digital tokenAn electronic record that someone can factually control: they can move it, block others from moving it, and prove they can do both1.s 761GB
PossessWhat counts is what a person can do in fact, not their legal rights1. A person does not jointly possess a token if they need someone else’s cooperation and that other person can act alone1.s 761GB(3), s 86
Digital asset platform (DAP)A facility where an operator possesses digital tokens for or on behalf of another person1.s 761GC
Tokenised custody platform (TCP)A facility where an operator holds an asset other than money and creates a single token for each asset. Holding the token gives a right to redeem the asset or direct its delivery1. A TCP cannot also be a DAP1.s 761GD
Public digital token infrastructure (PDTI)Open source, publicly accessible protocols that work without discretion, let any person contribute to running the network, and do not depend on one critical participant1. PDTI is not a financial product1.s 9E, s 765A
Platform rulesThe operator’s rules for a licensed platform. They must include transparent and non-discriminatory criteria for who can become a client1.s 912BG

What this means

“Possess” carries much of the weight. If investors alone control the keys to their tokens, nobody else possesses those tokens for them, so that part of an arrangement does not look like a DAP. If a business controls the keys for them, it may well be one1.

03

Licences, who is in and who is out

The operator of a platform needs one, unless an exemption fits. Several exemptions do.

What the law says

If a facility is a DAP or TCP and no exemption applies, the operator must hold an AFS licence that authorises it to issue that type of platform5. ASIC says related services, such as dealing in or advising about DAPs and TCPs, will also need a licence4.

The platform is a financial product in its own right, separate from the tokens or assets held through it5. Whether a token is itself a financial product is a different question, which ASIC answers in its existing guide, INFO 2255.

Small digital asset platforms. The Act exempts the issue of a DAP when four conditions are all met: none of the group’s platforms holds financial products; transactions through them total no more than $10 million over the last 12 months; whenever a token is added for a client, the client’s holdings across the platforms total no more than $5,000, with each token valued at the time it was added; and the operator has lodged a notice with ASIC1. The Government called these “smaller, low-risk platforms”2.

Regulations can raise those amounts1, and ASIC’s draft guide says the tests will rise with inflation5. The exemption is written for digital asset platforms only1.

Pointing people to a platform. A business that only tells people a DAP or TCP exists, or arranges for them to use one, does not need a licence for that if it does so in the ordinary course of its business and those services are not a significant part of the business1.

Validators. Running consensus on public digital token infrastructure is not a financial service, and the infrastructure itself is not a financial product1.

Exemptions still to come. ASIC expects Government regulations to extend some existing custody exemptions to DAPs and TCPs, subject to Government processes5. An exemption from licensing does not stop an arrangement being a DAP or TCP5.

Arrangements outside the definitions. Merely possessing tokens is not enough; they must be possessed for a client5. A facility may also fall outside both definitions if the client cannot direct how the assets are dealt with5, although it may then be another kind of financial product5.

A rough self-check

  1. Do you, or someone acting for you, possess digital tokens for another person? That describes a DAP1.
  2. Do you hold an asset other than money and create one token for it that lets the holder redeem it? That describes a TCP1.
  3. Does an exemption fit, such as the small platform exemption or the advise and arrange exemption1?
  4. If not, the operator needs an AFS licence5. A business already operating must apply by 7 October 20274.

This is a summary to help you find the right questions, not a legal test. ASIC’s draft guide says that if you are unsure, seek legal or regulatory advice5.

The dates that matter

Royal Assent in April 2026, a year’s wait, then six months for existing businesses to apply.

  13. 8 April to 7 October 2027Six-month transition period for businesses already operating14

During the transition period, a business already providing these services can apply to ASIC. If it does, the new rules do not apply to that service until the day after ASIC decides the application1. If it does not apply in time, the new rules apply as soon as the transition period ends1.

ASIC is considering publishing a list of firms that apply by 7 October 2027, so the public can see who may keep operating while their application is decided4. ASIC says it has already recorded over 140 applications from businesses seeking to provide digital asset-related financial services3.

8 April or 9 April 2027?

Some documents give 9 April 2027 as the start date, including ASIC’s roadmap, an earlier ASIC news item and the TASK-30 brief6720.

The Act sets its start as the day after the end of the 12 months beginning on the day of Royal Assent1. Royal Assent was given on 8 April 20261, so the 12 months run to 7 April 2027 and the Act starts on 8 April 2027. The commencement table records 8 April 20271; that column is not formally part of the Act1, but it matches the rule. ASIC’s October 2026 documents also use 8 April 202734. This guide uses 8 April 2027.

What this means

ASIC says the Act provides for an 18-month implementation timeline6. On this guide’s reading, that is the 12 months before the Act starts plus the six-month transition1.

05

Issuers on Tokeniser, and what actually changes for them

For many issuers, less than expected. The new rules mostly land on whoever holds tokens or assets for investors.

What the law says

Tokenising an asset does not by itself create a TCP. ASIC’s draft guide says not every arrangement that tokenises an existing asset is one5. Its examples include a token for a mixed pool of assets, and an arrangement where the operator makes investment decisions for the client5.

For funds, ASIC’s draft view is direct: where each token is a unit in a unit trust and the blockchain is the primary register of unit holders, the arrangement would generally be a managed investment scheme rather than a TCP5. A platform that is a managed investment scheme is not one of the two new products1.

Where a TCP is used, its underlying assets can include financial products, including tokens that are financial products5. For a TCP to stay outside the managed investment scheme rules, the Act requires, among other things, that all its underlying assets are of the same class and that a token is only as divisible as the asset can be physically delivered in parts1.

An issuer that makes a securities offer needing a disclosure document through a DAP or TCP must promptly tell the platform operator and each custodian about key events1, such as a supplementary or replacement document being lodged1. The disclosure document does not have to describe the platform itself1.

Any licensed platform must have platform rules with transparent, non-discriminatory eligibility criteria, and must tell potential clients whether and how their purchases will be recorded in any legal or technical register1.

What the companies say

Tokeniser says it operates on Redbelly Network, which it calls Australia’s public digital token infrastructure16, and that funds can run their registry and investor lifecycle on it19.

It describes its product as “engineered to align with Australia’s Digital Assets Framework, operating under your existing licence”18. It says trades that fail an eligibility check do not proceed18, and that eligibility, identity and the target market determination are enforced at the point of transfer17.

Asked in its technology FAQ whether a new licence is needed to use the infrastructure, Tokeniser answers “No”, saying the protocol layer is designed to sit outside markets, clearing and settlement, and custody licensing17. These are the company’s own statements about its product.

What this means

For a fund manager that uses Tokeniser to keep its unit register on chain, ASIC’s draft view suggests the fund remains a managed investment scheme5. The rules that already govern the fund keep applying, and the new platform regime is not the main question for the fund itself.

The new regime matters at the edges. If the manager, Tokeniser or a wallet provider controls investors’ tokens for them, that party could be operating a DAP. If someone holds a separate asset and issues one redeemable token for it, that could be a TCP1.

Questions an issuer may want to put to its own advisers

  • Who controls the private keys for investors’ tokens: the investor, the issuer, Tokeniser or a custodian?
  • Is the on-chain record the legal register of holders, or a copy of a register kept elsewhere?
  • Does anyone hold an underlying asset and issue one redeemable token for it?
  • Who makes the investment decisions?
  • Are the eligibility rules transparent and non-discriminatory, and do they match the fund’s target market?
  • How will secondary trading happen, and who runs that venue?

Not settled

The Tokeniser pages this guide relied on do not say who controls investors’ keys, so whether any part of the service is a DAP cannot be told from them. Its “no new licence” answer also rests on Redbelly being public digital token infrastructure, which the next section shows is not settled.

06

Redbelly against the new rules, what fits and what is open

Strong on identity and eligibility. The open question is whether the network itself counts as public digital token infrastructure.

What the companies say

Redbelly describes Receptor as its native digital identity and verifiable credential protocol10. Its verifier module is used to “define eligibility criteria” and check users’ credentials10, so that only eligible users can access particular products and services10.

Every participant must prove who they are before gaining access to the network8. For an access credential, a user proves through an accredited issuer that they hold a valid passport, checked biometrically9. Businesses can be verified too8.

The Eligibility SDK lets developers run checks such as age and KYC using privacy-preserving proofs11, and the onboarding SDK covers wallet authentication, KYC and on-chain permission setup12.

Redbelly states that “Redbelly is Digital Public Token Infrastructure”14 and that its finality means transactions cannot be reversed14. It was selected for the Reserve Bank of Australia’s Project Acacia pilot15.

Not settled

The Act’s second test asks whether the protocols “permit any person to contribute” to running the infrastructure1. ASIC’s draft guidance says infrastructure where “only select persons” can conduct consensus is not PDTI, and that this could include KYC restrictions; minimum staking or hardware requirements that apply to everyone are unlikely to count against it4. ASIC gives public permissionless blockchains as an example of PDTI4.

On Redbelly’s own pages, node operator applicants “will be selected based upon their previous experience, location and commitment to the Redbelly Project”13, and applications were closed when this guide checked13. Every network participant must also prove their identity8. The 100,000 RBNT stake applies to all nodes13, which is the kind of universal threshold ASIC’s draft says is unlikely to matter.

Two different things are being gated here. Identity checks for users decide who can transact; selection of node operators decides who can help run the network. The PDTI test is about the second, so node selection is the closer fit to ASIC’s concern, while the KYC point could matter too because ASIC names it.

No regulator has said whether Redbelly is PDTI in any source this guide found, and ASIC’s guidance is still a draft. ASIC also says licensing is assessed at a point in time, not on a future expected state4, so the answer could change as the network’s design changes.

Why it matters: if Redbelly is PDTI, running it is not a financial service and the network is not a financial product1. If it is not, activities on and around it would have to be assessed under the ordinary rules. That would not mean anyone is acting unlawfully. It means the answer is not yet known.

07

What holders get from the new rules

The protections attach to platforms that hold assets for you.

What the law says

A licensed operator must give a retail client a DAP/TCP Guide before issuing the platform to them1. It must include what a person would reasonably need to understand the platform and its risks1, and the method and extent of all charges1.

The platform rules act as a contract under seal between the licensee and each client1.

ASIC’s standards must require money held under a platform, other than its underlying assets, to be held in trust for clients1. They must not stop an operator holding many clients’ tokens in one wallet if its own accounts identify which tokens are each client’s1.

The Government listed access to dispute resolution and compensation among the core obligations that come with the licence2.

What this means

A holder who keeps their own keys is not a client of any platform for those tokens, so these protections do not apply to them. The protections matter when someone else holds the keys.

08

What is still open

Several pieces arrive between now and April 2027.

What this means

  • ASIC’s final guide and standards, planned for March 20274.
  • Government regulations for further exemptions5.
  • The financial requirements for licensees, including net tangible assets, which are part of ASIC’s consultation3.
  • Whether identity-gated networks with selected node operators, such as Redbelly, count as PDTI4.
  • Whether ASIC will publish a list of businesses that applied in time4.
  • Anyone can comment on ASIC’s proposals until 5pm AEDT on 27 November 20263.
